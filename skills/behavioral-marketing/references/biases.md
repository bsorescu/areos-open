# Bias Catalog — 16½ Biases That Influence Buying

Reference for stage 4 (behavioral pass). Based on Richard Shotton's
behavioral science research. Every "Pattern" below is raw material — claims
built from it still pass stage 5 (guardrails) before shipping.


## Contraindications — when NOT to use each bias

| # | Bias | Do NOT use when |
|---|------|-----------------|
| 1 | Habit Formation | The action is one-off (no cycle to build); uncertain rewards in trust-critical or regulated flows (feels like gambling) |
| 2 | Make It Easy | Removing friction that serves comprehension or consent (GDPR consent, legal acknowledgments must stay explicit) |
| 3 | Make It Difficult | Onboarding critical paths; users in a hurry or under stress; friction that gates value discovery |
| 4 | Generation Effect | High-intent users ready to buy (quizzes delay conversion); audiences answering under time pressure |
| 5 | Keats Heuristic | Regulated/B2B liability domains (rhyme undercuts gravitas); legal or pricing claims |
| 6 | Concreteness | Never contraindicated — but concrete claims must still be sourced |
| 6½ | Precision | Numbers you cannot source; false precision is a fabricated claim |
| B | Base Value Neglect | Sophisticated procurement buyers who compute unit prices; anywhere the math will be checked publicly |
| 7 | Extremeness Aversion | Single-product offers; audiences that comparison-shop on one dimension only |
| 8 | Rule of 100 | Mixed-currency audiences where % vs absolute reads differently; regulated price displays |
| 9 | Need to Experiment | Sample too small to reach significance — don't theater-test; decide and document instead |
| 10 | Framing (loss) | Unverified losses; fear-based domains where amplification erodes trust (see b2b-compliance.md); loyal existing customers |
| 11 | Fairness | "Because" with a hollow reason in front of expert buyers — they notice |
| 12 | Freedom of Choice | Mandatory changes (be definitive instead); legally required actions |
| 13 | Red Sneakers | Brand without established credibility; conservative/regulated audiences |
| 14 | Halo Effect | As a substitute for substance with expert buyers; polish can't carry unsourced claims |
| 15 | Wisdom of Wit | Risk/liability/incident contexts; detractor audiences; anywhere the joke touches the failure mode your product prevents |
| 16 | Peak-End Rule | Engineering a "peak" that overpromises what the product sustains post-purchase |

## Bias Details & Patterns

### 1. Habit Formation
**Fresh Start Effect:** People are more receptive to change at the start of cycles. Gym visits +47% at new term start. Reframe any date as a fresh start ("first day of spring" tripled sign-ups vs "third Thursday in March").

**Uncertain Rewards:** Intermittent rewards embed behavior more than certain ones (70% vs 43%). Random surprise discounts > predictable loyalty stamps. Pret gives random free coffees instead of stamp cards.

**Implementation Intentions:** "When X, I'll do Y" format. Increased exercise from 38% to 91%. Create specific cues tied to existing behavior.

**Pattern — Onboarding:**
- Let user pick their "start date" for going digital (commitment device)
- Random reward at onboarding completion (spin wheel, surprise discount)
- Tie actions to existing routines ("Every Monday morning, check your dashboard")

### 2. Make It Easy
**Friction Asymmetry:** People massively underestimate friction's impact. Auto-enroll with opt-out = 97%. Opt-in form = 1%. Every field you remove increases conversion.

**Foot-in-the-Door:** Small commitment first → large commitment later. Tiny sticker → large yard sign: 17% → 76% compliance. Trial → first building → invite colleague → subscribe.

**Choice Paralysis:** 24 options → 1.7% buy. 6 options → 12% buy. But only when preferences are undefined and options are similar.

**Pattern — Sign-up:**
- Minimum fields (email + password, nothing else)
- Pre-populate from public data (company lookup by tax ID)
- Show progress bar ("Step 2 of 3")
- Pre-built demo data so user sees value immediately

### 3. Make It Difficult (Strategic Friction)
**IKEA Effect:** Self-assembled = 63% higher valuation. Betty Crocker cake mix failed when too easy; succeeded when they added cracking an egg.

**Illusion of Effort:** Showing work increases perceived quality by 8-22%. Travel sites showing "searching 247 flights..." rated higher than instant results. Open kitchens → food rated 22% higher.

**Pattern — Quality Perception:**
- Show loading states with descriptive text ("Scanning document for viruses...", "Checking 47 legal requirements...")
- Display effort metrics: years of development, lines of code, tests, technologies used
- "Behind the scenes" section showing engineering rigor

### 4. Generation Effect
**Self-generated = sticky.** Fill-in-the-blank brand names remembered 14% better. Questions boost persuasion 14%. Oblique messages are more memorable than direct.

**Pattern — Landing Page:**
- Interactive calculator ("How many buildings? → Your price per building")
- Quiz ("Are you compliant with [regulation]?" → score → CTA)
- Ask questions, don't make claims ("What would one missed deadline cost you?")

### 5. Keats Heuristic (Rhyme as Reason)
Rhyme = 17% more believable, 2x more memorable. Alliteration = 7% more believable, 22% more memorable. Usage in ads has halved — opportunity.

**Pattern:** Use rhyme/alliteration in slogans, taglines, CTAs. "If the gloves don't fit, you must acquit" — devastating because it rhymes.

### 6. Concreteness
Concrete phrases are 10x more memorable. "1,000 songs in your pocket" beats "5GB storage." Individual stories beat statistics ($2.83 vs $1.17 in donations).

**Pattern — All Copy:**
- Replace abstractions: "comprehensive solution" → "tracks 47 document types across all your buildings"
- Use per-unit pricing: "€0.66 per apartment per month" beats "€19/month"
- Tell one customer's story, not aggregate statistics
- Check reading level — aim for age 13-14, not 17+

### 6½. Precision
Precise numbers are ~10% more credible. Asking for 17 cents = 60% more likely to receive money than asking for a quarter.

**Pattern:** "1,847 documents scanned" not "thousands of documents." "4 minutes 23 seconds average setup" not "quick setup." Precise pricing ($4.85) perceived as better value than round ($5.00).

### B. Base Value Neglect
"50% extra free" outsells equivalent "35% discount" by 81%. People add percentages naively (20% + 25% = 45%, feels better than 40%).

**Pattern — Promotions:**
- Annual plan: "2 months free" not "17% discount"
- Stacked: "20% off + additional 15% for new customers"
- Ascending order: small discount first, bigger second

### 7. Extremeness Aversion
Middle option chosen by default. Adding a super-premium tier doubled selection of the premium tier (18% → 37%). Stronger for utilitarian products and older audiences.

**Pattern — Pricing Page:**
- List most expensive first (left-to-right or top-to-bottom)
- Highlight the plan you want to sell as "Most Popular" or "Recommended"
- Ensure 3-4 tiers so the target tier sits in the middle
- Add a clearly inferior option near your target (decoy effect)
- Color-code: premium = highlight color, others = muted

### 8. Denominator Neglect (Rule of 100)
Under $100: percentage discounts feel larger. Over $100: absolute discounts feel larger. Font size matters: larger font for original price, smaller for sale price = +25% purchase likelihood.

**Pattern — Discounts:**
- €49 plan: "Save 30%" (not "Save €14.70")
- €199 plan: "Save €191 vs buying individually" (not "Save 49%")
- Display original price in larger font, sale price in smaller
- "Was 44% higher" outsells "Now 31% lower" (same math, 2x sales)

### 9. Need to Experiment
Experts can't predict what works. Music shifted wine purchases 83% but 86% of buyers denied any influence. Always test.

**Pattern:** A/B test everything. Use temporal reframing (daily price rated 4x better than annual). Monadic testing for survey-based decisions. Field experiments for real-world validation.

### 10. Framing
"75% lean" rated 31% higher than "25% fat." Loss framing 56% more effective than gain framing. Nouns (identity) > verbs (action).

**Pattern — Copy:**
- Loss frame: "Don't lose €X/month" not "Save €X/month"
- Nouns: "Be a compliant building" not "Comply with regulations"
- Shortage: "Sold out" not "Unavailable" (implies popularity, not failure)
- Temporal: smallest unit possible ("€0.63/day" not "€19/month")

### 11. Fairness
People reject unfair deals even at personal cost. The word "because" boosts compliance from 60% to 93%, even with a meaningless reason. Higher perceived costs = accepted higher prices.

**Pattern — Pricing Justification:**
- Always provide a "because": "€99/month because we maintain 200K lines of code, run 102 automated tests, and deploy weekly updates"
- Show the effort behind the product (resort hotel charges more for same beer)
- After competitor missteps, target their unhappy customers
- Watching eyes effect: images of eyes reduce antisocial behavior 35%

### 12. Freedom of Choice (Reactance)
"But you are free to refuse" = 5x compliance. Assertive messages backfire with loyal customers (-20% likeability). Definitive mandates are accepted better than tentative ones.

**Pattern — Upgrade Prompts:**
- "Just a suggestion — you're free to stay on your current plan"
- "No obligations. Cancel anytime."
- Let users advise on features (advisory input increases compliance 30%)
- Avoid "BUY NOW!" with existing customers
- If mandating a change, be definitive, not tentative

### 13. Red Sneakers Effect
Convention-breaking signals status — but ONLY if you already have it. Professor in t-shirt rated 14% more competent (at prestigious university); 8% LESS competent at low-ranking one. Must look deliberate, not accidental.

**Pattern — Brand Positioning:**
- Only break conventions if your brand has established credibility
- "We don't do free tiers. We don't do discounts. We build the best tool."
- Premium pricing itself signals intentional nonconformity
- Target audiences familiar with industry norms (they'll recognize the signal)

### 14. Halo Effect
One positive trait colors all perceptions. Attractive ATMs rated easier to use (no actual correlation). Typo on sign → 17% think product range is worse. Strongest for unknown brands and intangible attributes.

**Pattern — Design & Polish:**
- Invest in visual design — it halos into perceived quality and trust
- Fix every typo, broken link, misaligned element (negative halo is real)
- Boost likeability (humor, warmth) — it halos into trustworthiness
- For new brands: one standout positive trait matters more than broad adequacy

### 15. Wisdom of Wit
Humor: +44% memory, +11% exam scores, +37% perceived status. Declining in ads (53% → 34%) = opportunity. Works best with existing fans; backfires with detractors.

**Pattern — Tone of Voice:**
- Empty states: "No expired documents. Your inspector would be proud."
- Error messages: "Something went wrong. But it's not the end of the world — try again."
- Emails: conversational, not corporate
- 404 page: make it memorable
- Caveat: humor must align with core message, not distract from it

### 16. Peak-End Rule
Memory = peak moment + final moment. Negative peaks weigh 2x more than positive. Surprising moments feel 12% longer (oddball effect).

**Pattern — Trial Experience:**
- **Fill troughs first** (priority): eliminate worst friction points
- **Create one peak**: first "wow" moment (e.g., first generated report, first prevented issue)
- **End on a high**: trial expiry email should celebrate what they accomplished, not just demand payment
- Overestimate times → actual experience feels faster (Disney queue tactic)
- Surprise elements at unexpected moments (oddball effect)
